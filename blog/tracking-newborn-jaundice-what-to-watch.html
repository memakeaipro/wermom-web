Tracking newborn jaundice: what to watch in week one

Some mornings you unzip the swaddle, carry your baby to the window, and notice their skin looks a little more golden than yesterday. That first daylight glance is one of the most useful checks you can do in the newborn weeks — jaundice is far easier to see in natural light than under the warm glow of a nursery lamp. Most newborn jaundice is mild and clears on its own, but a handful of patterns mean a same-day phone call. Here's how to tell them apart without spiraling.

Why morning light is the best jaundice check you have

Jaundice is a yellow tint to the skin and the whites of the eyes. Indoor lighting — especially the warm, yellow-toned bulbs common in nurseries — can mask it or fake it. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) recommends assessing a newborn for jaundice in well-lit conditions, ideally natural daylight, which is exactly what a window in the morning gives you.

The reason a daily look matters: jaundice typically appears and changes over the first several days of life, often after you've already gone home. The AAP's 2022 clinical guidance emphasizes that the early days are when bilirubin tends to peak, so a parent's consistent eye is a genuine part of the safety net between newborn visits.

What jaundice actually is

Jaundice comes from bilirubin, a yellow pigment produced when the body breaks down old red blood cells. Newborns make bilirubin faster than adults and their immature livers clear it more slowly, so a mild buildup in the first week is extremely common. According to the AAP, roughly 6 in 10 full-term newborns and an even higher share of preterm babies develop some visible jaundice — making it one of the most ordinary findings in the nursery, not a rare emergency.

The pigment shows up first in places where it's easiest to see: the whites of the eyes and the face. As levels rise, the yellow tends to spread downward across the body, which is the basis of the head-to-toe check below.

“Most newborn jaundice is the liver simply catching up. Watch the timeline and the direction it spreads — that's what tells the story.”

The normal timeline: when it shows up and fades

Timing is one of the most important clues, because when jaundice appears changes what it likely means.

The common, low-risk pattern — often called physiological jaundice — usually becomes visible around day two or three of life, peaks somewhere between day three and day five, and then fades over the following week or two. In breastfed babies it can linger a bit longer, sometimes for several weeks, and still be benign.

The pattern that needs attention is jaundice that shows up in the first 24 hours of life. Per the AAP and NHS, yellowing that early is never assumed to be the harmless kind and is always evaluated promptly. The same goes for jaundice that keeps deepening past the first week or appears for the first time after the baby is a week old.

How to check at home, head to toe

You don't need a device. A simple, repeatable look in good light does most of the work.

The press test. With a clean finger, gently press on your baby's skin — the forehead or the tip of the nose is easy — then lift your finger and look at the spot before the color returns. If the skin that blanches looks yellow rather than pale, that's jaundice. This works on all skin tones, where the eye alone can be unreliable; on darker skin, checking the whites of the eyes, the gums, and the palms and soles is especially helpful.

Follow the direction. Jaundice generally moves head-downward as bilirubin rises. Yellowing limited to the face is usually milder; yellow that has reached the chest, belly, and especially the legs and feet suggests a higher level and is a reason to call. Tracking how far down the color has spread from one morning to the next is more informative than any single glance.

Important caveat: home looking estimates trend, not a number. Skin assessment can under- or over-estimate the true bilirubin level, which is why any concern is confirmed with a proper measurement — a light meter on the skin or a blood test — at the clinic. Your job at home is to notice change early and report it, not to diagnose severity.

Feeding, jaundice, and the diaper connection

Feeding and jaundice are closely linked, which is why this is so worth tracking together. Bilirubin leaves the body largely through stool, so a baby who is feeding well and passing plenty of dirty and wet diapers is actively clearing it. A baby who isn't feeding enough — common in the first days while breastfeeding is still being established — can become more jaundiced and more dehydrated at the same time.

The AAP's practical advice is reassuringly hands-on: for most jaundiced newborns, frequent, effective feeding is part of the management. That usually means breastfeeding around eight to twelve times a day in the early period, or following your pediatrician's guidance on formula amounts. Counting wet and dirty diapers gives you a second, independent signal that feeding is on track — a drop in output alongside deepening yellow is exactly the combination worth flagging quickly.

One myth worth retiring: sunlight through a window is not a treatment for jaundice. It doesn't deliver the right kind of light safely and risks overheating or sunburn. Real phototherapy uses controlled medical lights under supervision.

What to log in week one

A useful jaundice log is short. The point is to make a trend visible, not to turn the morning into a medical exam:

  • Day of life — timing is the single most important clue
  • Where you see yellow — eyes only, face, chest, belly, or down to legs/feet
  • Direction of change — less than yesterday, the same, or spreading further down
  • Feeds — roughly how many in 24 hours, and whether the baby is alert and feeding eagerly
  • Wet and dirty diapers — a daily count, your best at-home hydration signal
  • Behavior — normal and rousable, or unusually sleepy and hard to wake for feeds

What to skip: agonizing over the exact shade, or comparing your baby's color to a photo on the internet. The pattern over days, paired with feeding and alertness, is what actually guides decisions.

The signs that mean call today — or go now

Most jaundice is mild, but high bilirubin levels left unchecked can, rarely, become dangerous — which is why pediatric guidance is firm about not waiting on certain signs. Contact your pediatrician promptly if you notice:

  • Jaundice in the first 24 hours of life — always evaluated, never assumed benign
  • Yellow spreading to the belly, arms, legs, or feet, or deepening day over day
  • Poor feeding — not waking for feeds, sucking weakly, or far fewer wet diapers
  • An unusually sleepy or floppy baby who is hard to rouse
  • Jaundice that lasts beyond about two weeks, or appears for the first time after day 7
  • Pale, chalky stools or very dark urine alongside the yellowing

Seek urgent, same-day care for the more serious end: a high-pitched cry, arching of the back or neck, stiffness or limpness, fever, or a baby who simply isn't feeding and is increasingly difficult to wake. These are uncommon, but they are the reason the early checks exist. When in doubt, a phone call to your pediatrician is always the right move — describe the timeline, how far the yellow has spread, and how feeding is going.

Treatment and the reassuring big picture

When jaundice does need treatment, the most common approach is phototherapy: the baby rests under special blue-spectrum lights that help break bilirubin down into a form the body can clear more easily. It's a routine, well-established treatment, often done over a day or two, and the large majority of babies who need it do completely fine.

Step back and the picture is genuinely reassuring. Newborn jaundice is one of the most common conditions in the first week of life, the benign physiological form is by far the most frequent, and the dangerous extreme is rare precisely because the system — your daily look, the newborn weight and bilirubin checks, the pediatrician's follow-up — is built to catch it early. Track the timeline, follow the direction the yellow spreads, keep feeds and diapers strong, and call with specifics when something shifts. That's the whole job.
