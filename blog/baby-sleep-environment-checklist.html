Baby Sleep Environment Checklist: Safe and Settled

By the Wermom Editorial Team · Evidence-checked against AAP, AASM, NHS & CDC guidance

Before you blame the 5 a.m. wake-ups on your baby, it's worth looking at the room. A sleep environment that's too bright, too warm, or cluttered with things that don't belong in a crib can quietly sabotage otherwise good sleep — and a couple of those things are safety issues, not just comfort ones.

Here's a practical, head-to-toe checklist for the space your baby actually sleeps in. The safety items are non-negotiable; the comfort items are where you get to experiment.

The non-negotiables (safety first)

These come straight from AAP and CDC safe-sleep guidance, and there's no flexibility here:

  • A firm, flat surface. A crib, bassinet, or play yard that meets current safety standards — not a couch, an adult bed, or an inclined seat.
  • A bare sleep space. Fitted sheet only. No pillows, blankets, bumpers, stuffed animals, or positioners. They're a suffocation risk.
  • Back to sleep. Every nap, every night, until age one.
  • In your room for the first six months, on their own separate surface. (CDC: Sudden Infant Death — Safe Sleep)

If your setup fails any of those four, fix that before you touch anything else on this list.

Temperature: cooler than you think

Overheating raises the risk of SIDS, so the goal is a room that's comfortable for a lightly dressed adult — not toasty. Dress your baby in one layer more than you'd wear, and use a wearable sleep sack instead of a loose blanket for warmth. Skip hats indoors. If your baby's chest or the back of their neck feels sweaty, they're too warm. (AAP – HealthyChildren.org: Safe Sleep)

Light: dark for sleep, bright for waking

Babies, like adults, take their cues about day and night partly from light.

  • For sleep: make the room as dark as you reasonably can. Blackout curtains help, especially for early-evening bedtimes and summer naps.
  • A dim, warm-colored night light is fine for feeds and diaper changes — keep it low and avoid bright white or blue light, which is more alerting.
  • In the morning and during wake windows, open things up. Daylight exposure helps your baby's body clock settle into a day-night rhythm over the early months.

Sound: steady, not silent

A tomb-quiet house can actually make things harder, because every creak stands out. A steady, low background sound — a fan or a white-noise machine — can smooth over household noise. Keep any sound machine at a low volume and across the room rather than right next to your baby's head, and not turned up loud.

Putting it together: the quick checklist

Run through this before each bedtime for the first few months:

  • [ ] Baby on their back, on a firm flat surface
  • [ ] Crib bare — sheet only
  • [ ] Sleep sack on, no loose blanket
  • [ ] Room comfortably cool, baby not overbundled
  • [ ] Room dark; night light dim if needed
  • [ ] Background sound low and steady (optional)
  • [ ] Baby's surface in your room (first six months)
A note on this guide: This is general educational information based on AAP and CDC safe-sleep guidance, not medical advice for your specific baby. Check with your pediatrician about anything specific to your child.

Frequently asked questions

Is a white-noise machine safe for babies?

It can be helpful at a low volume, placed away from your baby rather than right beside the crib. Keep the volume modest and not running at high levels all night.

What temperature should the room be?

There's no single magic number — aim for what's comfortable for a lightly dressed adult and dress your baby in one extra layer. Watch for signs of overheating rather than chasing an exact degree.

Can I put a crib by the window?

Keep the crib away from windows, blind cords, and curtains, which are strangulation hazards. A spot away from drafts and cords is safest.
