Night Weaning: When Your Baby Is Ready and How to Do It Gently

By the Wermom Editorial Team · Evidence-checked against AAP, AASM, NHS & CDC guidance

There's a particular kind of tired that comes from feeding a baby who, somewhere along the way, stopped being hungry at night and started feeding mostly out of habit and comfort. If you're wondering whether it's time to drop those overnight feeds — this is your guide. We'll cover how to know if your baby's ready, when to absolutely wait, and how to wean nights without the drama.

First, the most important rule of the whole topic.

Don't night wean a baby who still needs the calories

Night feeds exist for a reason, and for a good chunk of the first year that reason is nutrition. Young infants genuinely need to feed overnight, and weaning too early can affect weight gain and milk supply. The single best move before you change anything is to talk to your pediatrician and confirm your baby is growing well and no longer medically needs those overnight calories.

This matters for nursing parents in particular: overnight feeds play a real role in maintaining milk supply, especially in the early months. The Academy of Breastfeeding Medicine notes that night feedings are normal and developmentally appropriate for infants, and that dropping them is an individual decision to make with your provider — not a milestone every baby should hit on a schedule (Academy of Breastfeeding Medicine – protocols).

Signs your baby may be ready

There's no magic date, but readiness usually shows up as a combination of:

  • Your baby is past the early months and gaining weight well, confirmed by your pediatrician.
  • They're eating well during the day (and, if age-appropriate, taking solids).
  • The night feeds have become brief and snacky — a few minutes of comfort sucking rather than a real, hungry feed.
  • They fall back asleep easily, suggesting the waking is more about the feed-to-sleep association than genuine hunger.

If your baby is taking full, hungry feeds overnight and is on the younger side, they're probably not ready yet — and that's completely normal.

A gentle, gradual approach

The kindest way to night wean for most families is slowly, so your baby (and your body, if nursing) adjust without distress.

1. Trim, don't cut. Rather than dropping a feed overnight, shorten it. If nursing, reduce time at the breast by a couple of minutes every few nights. If bottle-feeding, reduce the amount gradually. Slowly the feed shrinks to nothing.

2. Add comfort that isn't food. As feeds shrink, your baby will need another way to resettle. A consistent calming routine, white noise, a hand on the chest, or (for older babies) a comfort object can fill the gap. This is also a good moment to gently practice "drowsy but awake" — the AAP recommends putting babies down awake so they learn to fall asleep in their own sleep space rather than only via feeding (HealthyChildren.org – AAP).

3. Shift the last feed earlier. Move the final feed to the start of the bedtime routine instead of the very last step, so feeding stops being the thing that tips your baby into sleep.

4. Lean on the other parent for wakings, if you have one. Many babies feed less at night for someone who can't nurse them — a partner offering comfort instead of milk can ease the transition significantly.

5. Go at a humane pace. There's no prize for speed. The NHS underlines that babies differ enormously and sleep changes unfold differently for each one (NHS – Helping your baby to sleep). If a step feels too hard one week, hold steady and try again later.

If you're nursing: protect your supply and comfort

Dropping feeds too fast can leave you engorged or risk a blocked duct, and can dent supply. Wean gradually, and if your breasts feel uncomfortably full overnight, express just enough for comfort. If you notice supply dropping more than you'd like during the day, slow the night-weaning pace.

A reassuring frame

Night weaning isn't a test you pass or fail, and a baby who still needs night feeds isn't "behind." Many babies naturally drop overnight feeds on their own as they grow, with no formal effort at all. You're allowed to wait until you feel ready too — exhaustion is a perfectly valid reason to begin, and contentment is a perfectly valid reason to wait.

A note on this guide: This is general educational information reviewed against AAP, Academy of Breastfeeding Medicine and NHS guidance — not medical advice. Always confirm with your pediatrician that your baby is gaining well and no longer needs overnight calories before night weaning.

Frequently asked questions

At what age can I night wean?

There's no universal age. Readiness depends on your baby's growth and feeding, confirmed by your pediatrician — not the calendar. Many babies aren't ready until well into the first year or beyond.

Will night weaning hurt my milk supply?

It can if done too fast. Wean gradually, express for comfort if engorged, and slow down if your daytime supply dips. Talk to an IBCLC or your provider if you're concerned.

My baby still wakes after I night weaned. Did it fail?

Not necessarily. Weaning the feed and ending the waking are two different things — your baby may still surface but now needs comforting back to sleep without milk. That settling skill often takes a little longer to develop.
